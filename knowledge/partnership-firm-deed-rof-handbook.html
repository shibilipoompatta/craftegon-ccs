1. Indian Partnership Act, 1932: Statutory Architecture & Core Concepts

The Indian Partnership Act, 1932 (Act IX of 1932) defines the commercial and contractual relationship between individuals carrying on a business together. Unlike a corporate body formed under the Companies Act, 2013 or the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008, a traditional partnership firm is not a distinct juristic legal entity in civil law (Dulichand Laxminarayan v. CIT, AIR 1956 SC 354); it is a collective noun representing the partners composing it.

The Quintessential Triple Test of Partnership (Section 4 & Section 6)

Under Section 4, a partnership requires five cumulative elements: (i) an association of two or more persons; (ii) a voluntary contract; (iii) the conduct of a business; (iv) an agreement to share profits; and (v) Mutual Agency (“carried on by all or any of them acting for all”).

  • The “Real Relation” Doctrine (Section 6): Codifying the English rule in Cox v. Hickman (1860), sharing of profits is prima facie evidence but not conclusive proof of a partnership. Receipt of profit by money lenders, employees, widows, or sellers of goodwill does not make them partners. Mutual agency remains the acid test.
  • Contract, Not Status (Section 5): Partnership arises purely from contract. A Hindu Undivided Family (HUF) business is not a partnership, and coparceners are not partners merely by birth.
  • Partner Cap (Companies Act 2013 Section 464): Read with Rule 10 of the Companies (Miscellaneous) Rules 2014, any partnership firm exceeding 50 partners becomes an Illegal Association, exposing partners to personal penalties up to ₹1,00,000 and total forfeiture of judicial enforcement rights (Badri Prasad v. Nagarmal, AIR 1959 SC 559).

Section 25: Unlimited Joint & Several Liability

Every partner is liable, jointly with all other partners and also severally, for all acts of the firm done while they are a partner. Creditors can proceed against the private personal estate of any individual partner without first exhausting firm assets.

Section 19(2): Statutory Negative Restrictions

Unless authorized by deed or trade usage, a partner’s implied authority does NOT empower them to: submit disputes to arbitration, open bank accounts in their own name, compromise claims, withdraw suits, admit liability, acquire/transfer immovable property, or enter into another partnership on behalf of the firm.

2. Section 69 Disability & Landmark Supreme Court Precedents

Registration of a partnership firm with the Registrar of Firms (RoF) under Section 58 & 59 is voluntary at inception. However, Section 69 imposes severe civil disabilities on unregistered firms, making formal registration practically indispensable for commercial enterprise operations.

Statutory Provision Civil Prohibition Imposed Statutory Exceptions & Carve-Outs
Section 69(1) Bar on suits by a partner against the firm or co-partners to enforce contractual rights or rights under the Act. Suits for dissolution of the firm, accounts of a dissolved firm, or realizing assets of a dissolved firm (Sec 69(3)(a)).
Section 69(2) Bar on suits by or on behalf of an unregistered firm against any third party to enforce rights arising from a contract. Statutory rights (trademark/copyright infringement) and common law torts (passing off, eviction of trespassers).
Section 69(3) Bar on claiming set-offs or initiating other civil proceedings to enforce contractual claims. Set-off claims not exceeding ₹100 in Small Cause Courts; defending against third-party suits.

Binding Supreme Court Rulings on Section 69 Interpretation

1. Haldiram Bhujiawala v. Anand Kumar Deepak Kumar (2000) 3 SCC 250:
The Supreme Court authoritatively held that the phrase “arising from a contract” in Section 69(2) is strictly limited to commercial contracts entered into by the firm with third parties in the course of business. Section 69(2) does not bar statutory suits (e.g., trademark infringement under the Trade Marks Act) or common law tort actions (such as passing off or trespass).
2. V. Subramaniam v. Rajesh Raghuvandra Rao (2009) 5 SCC 608:
The Supreme Court struck down Section 69(2A) as introduced by the Maharashtra state legislature (which barred dissolution suits by unregistered firms) as unconstitutional and violative of Articles 14 and 19(1)(g). The right of a partner in an unregistered firm to seek dissolution, accounts, and asset distribution under Section 69(3)(a) is inviolable.
3. Sunkari Tirumala Rao v. Penki Aruna Kumari, 2025 INSC 92:
The Supreme Court reiterated in January 2025 that Section 69 is an exception to the common law right to judicial redress and must be construed strictly. Where the substantive relief in a partnership dispute relates to dissolution, accounts, or partition of venture assets, non-updation of reconstitution in the Register of Firms does not bar the suit under Section 69(1).

3. Partnership Deed Drafting & Essential Statutory Covenants

The Partnership Deed is the foundational constitutional charter governing the internal management, profit-sharing ratios, capital structures, and dispute resolution mechanisms of the firm. A poorly drafted deed creates severe civil deadlock and tax disallowances.

1. Working Partner Remuneration (Sec 40(b)(v))

Must explicitly designate specific individuals as “Working Partners” who actively devote time to the business. Remuneration must follow the statutory formula linked to Book Profit under the Finance Act 2024 (AY 2025-26): up to ₹3,00,000 or 90% of book profit on the first ₹6,00,000, and 60% on the balance.

2. Interest on Capital (Sec 40(b)(iv))

Must be explicitly authorized in the deed. The statutory ceiling for tax deductibility is simple interest at 12% per annum. Any rate paid in excess of 12% is compulsorily disallowed in the firm’s tax computation.

3. Non-Dissolution on Death (Sec 42(c) Carve-Out)

Under Section 42(c), a firm automatically dissolves upon the death of a partner unless contracted otherwise. The deed MUST contain an express non-dissolution covenant allowing surviving partners to continue the business and settle the deceased partner’s estate via book value or agreed goodwill valuation.

4. Express Banking & Operational Mandates

Expressly defines banking signing mandates (sole, joint, or multi-signature thresholds). Overrides Section 19(2) restrictions by explicitly delineating borrowing limits, credit transactions, and management powers.

4. Stamp Duty, Execution & The Legal Hierarchy

Stamp duty on partnership deeds is governed by State Stamp Acts under Entry 63, List II of the Constitution. Insufficiently stamped instruments are inadmissible in evidence under Section 35 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 and subject to impounding and 10x penalty.

Crucial Distinction: Notarisation is NOT RoF Registration

A common mistake among founders is believing that having a partnership deed notarized on stamp paper constitutes registration. Notarisation merely creates a rebuttable presumption of execution under Section 85 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. It does NOT constitute registration with the Registrar of Firms (RoF). An unregistered notarized firm remains subject to the complete civil litigation bars under Section 69!

5. Kerala eGroops 2.0 Online Registration (S.R.O. No. 255/2025)

In Kerala, the registration of partnership firms is governed by the Kerala Partnership (Registration of Firms) Rules, 1959, administered by the Registration Department, Government of Kerala. Effective 2025, manual submissions are completely prohibited pursuant to the Kerala Partnership Amendment Rules, 2025 (S.R.O. No. 255/2025, G.O.(P) No.35/2025/TAXES).

The eGroops 2.0 Digital Registration Workflow

  1. Deed Execution: Execute the Partnership Deed on ₹1,000 Kerala e-Stamp Paper with the signatures of all partners and two independent attesting witnesses.
  2. eGroops 2.0 Account Creation: Register on the official portal (egroops.kerala.gov.in) and initiate the Form A (Section 58) e-application.
  3. Form A Data Capture: Submit firm name, principal office, branch offices, partner dates of joining, and capital details. Pursuant to S.R.O. 255/2025, an Official Firm Email ID and Official Mobile Number are mandatory for automated RoF notices.
  4. Professional Attestation: Generate draft Form A. All partners sign the verification statement. It must be certified by an Authorized Attesting Officer (Advocate, Practicing Chartered Accountant, or Gazetted Officer) with their registration number.
  5. Document Upload: Upload color scans of the stamped Partnership Deed, attested Form A, partner Aadhaar/PAN, and principal business premises proof (Tax receipt, registered rent deed & landlord NOC).
  6. e-Treasury Remittance: Pay government fees via the integrated Kerala e-Treasury gateway.
  7. Scrutiny & Form C Issuance: Reviewed by the District Registrar (Firms). Upon approval within 5–7 business days, the statutory entry is recorded in the Register of Firms under Section 59, and Form C (Certificate of Registration) with a cryptographic QR code is issued.

6. Partnership Firm Taxation & Section 40(b) Deductions

Under Section 2(31)(iv) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, a partnership firm is assessed as a separate taxable entity. Total income is taxed at a flat rate, with statutory exemptions preventing double taxation on partner profit shares.

Key Tax Rules for Partners and Firms (AY 2025-26 / AY 2026-27)

  • Section 10(2A) Profit Share Exemption: The partner’s share in the total income of the firm is 100% tax-exempt in the partner’s personal hands, preventing double taxation.
  • Section 28(v) Remuneration & Interest: Remuneration and interest allowed as a deduction to the firm are taxed in the partner’s personal return under “Profits & Gains of Business or Profession”.
  • Section 44AB Tax Audit Threshold: Mandatory audit if turnover exceeds ₹1 Crore (raised to ₹10 Crores if aggregate cash receipts & payments are ≤ 5%). For professions, the threshold is ₹50 Lakhs (raised to ₹75 Lakhs if cash receipts ≤ 5%).
  • Presumptive Taxation (44AD / 44ADA): Eligible firms can declare 8% (6% digital) under 44AD or 50% under 44ADA, but no separate deduction is allowed for partner remuneration or interest under presumptive schemes.

7. Post-Registration Banking, Tax & Operational Compliance

Registration with the RoF completes the legal formation of the firm. Immediate post-registration steps are necessary to operationalize banking, indirect tax compliance, and commercial contracting.

1. Entity PAN & TAN

Apply for the firm's permanent PAN in Form 49A. If the firm makes payments subject to TDS (rent, contractor fees, professional fees), TAN application in Form 49B is mandatory.

2. Bank Current Account

Opened in the name of the firm backed by the registered Partnership Deed, RoF Form C, PAN card, and authorized signatory mandates.

3. GST Registration

Mandatory if aggregate turnover exceeds ₹40 Lakhs (goods) or ₹20 Lakhs (services), or immediately for interstate sales and e-commerce vendor activities.

4. MSME 43B(h) Rule

Timely payments to registered Micro and Small suppliers within 15 days (or agreed terms up to 45 days) to avoid disallowance of business deductions in the financial year.
