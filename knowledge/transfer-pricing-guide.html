TRANSFER PRICING & CROSS-BORDER TAXATION • INDIA

Complete Guide to Transfer Pricing and Form 3CEB Compliance in India

A comprehensive regulatory handbook navigating the arm's length principle under Sections 92 to 92F of the Income Tax Act, 1961, Associated Enterprise (AE) determination, benchmarking methodologies, mandatory Chartered Accountant certification in Form 3CEB, and Section 271G penalty defense.

Reviewed: November 2026 • Applicable for Assessment Year 2026–27 & ongoing cross-border corporate audits

1. The Regulatory Architecture of Transfer Pricing in India

Transfer pricing regulations in India are codified under Chapter X (Special Provisions Relating to Avoidance of Tax) comprising Sections 92 to 92F of the Income Tax Act, 1961, read in conjunction with Rules 10A to 10TH of the Income-tax Rules, 1962. Enacted through the Finance Act, 2001, and patterned on the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines, the statutory core mandate is encapsulated in Section 92(1): any income arising from an international transaction shall be computed having regard to the arm's length price (ALP).

The fundamental economic objective of Indian transfer pricing law is to prevent multinational enterprises (MNEs) and cross-border conglomerates from shifting taxable profits out of the Indian sovereign tax net through non-market, arbitrary internal pricing of goods, intangible assets, administrative services, management fees, royalty charges, or intra-group debt financing.

2. Associated Enterprise (AE) Determination: The 13 Statutory Deeming Tests

The prerequisite for the applicability of transfer pricing is the existence of two or more Associated Enterprises (AEs). Under Section 92A(1), an enterprise is deemed an AE if it participates, directly or indirectly, or through one or more intermediaries, in the management, control, or capital of the other enterprise. To remove ambiguity, Section 92A(2) specifies thirteen (13) objective, non-rebuttable statutory conditions where two enterprises are deemed to be AEs:

Section 92A(2) Clause Statutory Deeming Trigger Practical Corporate Example
Clause (a) & (b): Equity Holding Direct or indirect holding of shares carrying ≥ 26% of the voting power. A US Delaware parent entity holding 26% or more equity shares in an Indian software development subsidiary.
Clause (c): Debt & Asset Exposure Loan advanced by one enterprise constitutes ≥ 51% of the book value of total assets of the borrower. A Singapore entity providing term debt funding equal to 55% of an Indian tech startup's total asset ledger.
Clause (d): Loan Guarantees One enterprise guarantees ≥ 10% of total borrowings of the other enterprise. Foreign holding firm issuing an institutional corporate guarantee securing an Indian subsidiary's working capital credit.
Clause (e) & (f): Board Control Appointment of more than half of the board, or one or more executive/managing directors. Investor holding right to nominate the Managing Director or Chief Executive Officer of the Indian company.
Clause (g): Intangible Dependency Manufacturing/business depends wholly on patents, know-how, trademarks, or copyrights of the other. Indian manufacturer operating solely under proprietary industrial formula licensed from a German corporation.
Clause (h) & (i): Commercial Exclusivity Supply of ≥ 90% of raw materials, or sale under prices and conditions influenced by the other. Indian export company selling its entire product inventory exclusively to a single overseas distributor.

3. Scope of International Transactions & Specified Domestic Transactions (SDT)

Under Section 92B, an International Transaction is defined expansively to include any transaction between two or more associated enterprises (at least one of which is a non-resident) in the nature of:

  • Purchase, sale, lease, or transfer of tangible property (raw materials, capital machinery, commercial goods).
  • Purchase, sale, assignment, or license of intangible property (patents, copyrights, trademarks, software code, customer lists, brand goodwill).
  • Provision of technical, professional, marketing, back-office, or corporate administrative services.
  • Lending or borrowing of money, inter-company advances, cash-pooling, and commercial loan guarantees.
  • Cost-sharing arrangements for research & development (R&D) or shared infrastructure expenses.
  • Deemed International Transactions (Section 92B(2)): Any transaction between an Indian enterprise and an unrelated third party if there exists a prior agreement between the third party and the foreign AE, or if the relevant terms are determined in substance between them.
Specified Domestic Transactions (SDT - Section 92BA): Transfer pricing provisions also extend to domestic transactions between related Indian entities (e.g., transfers between tax-exempt units under Section 80-IA / 10AA and domestic taxable units), provided the aggregate value of such domestic transactions exceeds ₹20 Crore in the relevant financial year.

4. The 5 Prescribed Transfer Pricing Benchmarking Methods

Under Section 92C(1) read with Rule 10B, the Arm's Length Price (ALP) must be computed using the Most Appropriate Method (MAM) selected from the five statutory methods (or the "Other Method" prescribed under Rule 10AB):

1. Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) Method

Compares the price charged in a controlled transaction directly with the price charged in an uncontrolled transaction under identical circumstances.

Best suited for: Traded commodities, financial interest rates, standard market products.
2. Resale Price Method (RPM)

Evaluates the gross profit margin earned by a distributor who buys goods from an AE and resells them to unrelated parties without adding substantial manufacturing value.

Best suited for: Pure distributors, wholesale importers, resellers.
3. Cost Plus Method (CPM)

Computes the gross markup added to direct and indirect costs of production incurred by the enterprise in supplying goods or services to an AE.

Best suited for: Contract manufacturers, semi-finished goods suppliers, captive service centers.
4. Profit Split Method (PSM)

Allocates combined operating profit or loss arising from interrelated cross-border transactions based on relative economic contributions and functions performed.

Best suited for: Integrated global operations with shared unique intangibles (IP, patents).
5. Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM)

Examines the net operating profit margin relative to an appropriate base (costs, sales, assets) earned by the tested party compared to comparable uncontrolled companies.

Best suited for: IT services (software development), ITeS (BPO/KPO), technical support services (most widely applied in India).

5. Mandatory Chartered Accountant Certification in Form 3CEB (Section 92E)

Under Section 92E, every person who has entered into an international transaction or a specified domestic transaction during a previous year must obtain an independent audit report from a Chartered Accountant in Form 3CEB and furnish it electronically to the Income Tax Department.

Internal Architecture of Form 3CEB:

  • Part A (General Information): Taxpayer corporate profile, PAN, CIN, residential status, and aggregate value of international transactions and specified domestic transactions.
  • Part B (International Transactions): Detailed item-wise disclosure of transactions with AEs: sales/purchases of tangible property, intangible licensing, services rendered/received, loans, and corporate guarantees. For each transaction, the CA must verify the method used, arm's length price, and book value.
  • Part C (Specified Domestic Transactions): Details of domestic transactions exceeding statutory thresholds under Section 92BA, including business transfers and inter-unit supply pricing.
Statutory Filing Deadline: For corporate taxpayers subject to transfer pricing audit under Section 92E, the statutory deadline to file Form 3CEB on the Income Tax e-Filing portal is October 31st of the assessment year. The corporate income tax return (Form ITR-6) is consequently due on November 30th.

6. The Three-Tier Transfer Pricing Documentation Architecture (BEPS Action 13)

Following India's commitment to the OECD Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) Action Plan 13, Section 92D prescribes a comprehensive, three-tiered documentation structure:

  1. Tier 1: Local File (Rule 10D): Detailed entity-level documentation maintained contemporaneously by every taxpayer with cross-border AE transactions exceeding ₹1 Crore. Contains detailed Functional, Asset, and Risk (FAR) analysis, industry overview, economic benchmarking search strings, and database extracts (Prowess / Capitaline).
  2. Tier 2: Master File (Form 3CEAA): High-level overview of global MNE operations, global organizational structure, intangible asset ownership strategies, and intra-group financing policies. Mandatory for Indian entities belonging to an MNE group if: (a) Consolidated group revenue exceeds ₹500 Crore, AND (b) Value of international transactions exceeds ₹50 Crore (or intangible transactions exceed ₹10 Crore).
  3. Tier 3: Country-by-Country Report (CbCR - Form 3CEAD): Annual reporting of revenue, profit before tax, income tax paid, stated capital, accumulated earnings, employee headcounts, and tangible assets for each tax jurisdiction in which the group operates. Applies to international groups with consolidated revenue exceeding ₹6,400 Crore.

7. Statutory Penalties & Scrutiny Defense Framework

Non-compliance with transfer pricing reporting and documentation requirements attracts severe, non-discretionary statutory financial penalties under Chapter XXI of the Income Tax Act:

Section Nature of Contravention / Default Statutory Financial Penalty
Section 271BA Failure to furnish accountant's report in Form 3CEB before due date Flat ₹1,00,000
Section 271AA Failure to keep, maintain, or report any international transaction / Master File default 2% of the value of each international transaction (₹5,00,000 for Master File)
Section 271G Failure to furnish information or documents requisitioned by TPO / Assessing Officer 2% of the value of the international transaction
Section 92CE Secondary Adjustment: Failure to repatriate excess money into India within 90 days of primary TP adjustment Treated as an advance with recurring monthly imputed interest (SBI 1-year MCLR + 325 bps) or one-time 20.96% tax

Frequently Asked Questions: Transfer Pricing & Form 3CEB

Is Form 3CEB mandatory if the Indian company incurred an operating loss? Yes. The requirement to file Form 3CEB depends solely on the existence of an international transaction with an Associated Enterprise during the financial year. The profitability or loss status of the taxpayer is completely irrelevant for filing applicability.
What is the tolerance range (safe harbour band) for arm's length price adjustments? Under the third proviso to Section 92C(2), if the variation between the transaction price and the arm's length price does not exceed 1% for wholesale traders or 3% for all other taxpayers, the actual transaction price is accepted as the arm's length price without adverse tax adjustments.
Does issuance of shares or receipt of equity funding from a foreign parent require Form 3CEB? Yes. Capital financing transactions, including the issuance of equity shares, CCPS, or debentures to an Associated Enterprise, are international transactions under Section 92B and must be reported in Part B of Form 3CEB, along with valuation benchmarking.
Can an Indian startup opt for Safe Harbour Rules? Yes. Eligible entities providing software development or ITeS can opt for Safe Harbour under Rule 10TD/10TE by declaring an operating profit margin of 17% to 18% (depending on transaction size up to ₹200 Crore), filed in Form 3CEFA, avoiding transfer pricing audits.
Who can sign and certify Form 3CEB? Under Section 92E, Form 3CEB must be audited and digitally signed by an independent Chartered Accountant holding an active Certificate of Practice (CoP) from ICAI, and then accepted electronically by the authorized director using digital signature credentials.
