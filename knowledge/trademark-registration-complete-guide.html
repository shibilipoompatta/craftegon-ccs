Complete Statutory Guide to Trademark Registration in India

Author: IP Law Research Group Published: 16 November 2026 Status: Active Statutory Guidance Reading Time: 15 mins
TRADEMARK ACT 1999

Statutory Transparency & Professional Boundaries

Trademark applications in India are processed, examined, and granted exclusively by the Trade Marks Registry under the Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks (CGPDTM), DPIIT, Ministry of Commerce and Industry (ipindia.gov.in). Statutory government filing fees are ₹4,500 per mark per class for Individuals, Startups, and MSMEs, and ₹9,000 for large corporate bodies. CraftEgon Corporate Services (CCS) acts as an independent corporate advisory and registered trademark filing desk providing clearance searches, Form TM-A drafting, examination responses, and portfolio monitoring.

1. What is a Trademark? Section 2(1)(zb) Architecture

Under Section 2(1)(zb) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999, a "trade mark" is defined as a mark capable of being represented graphically and which is capable of distinguishing the goods or services of one person from those of others. It may include the shape of goods, their packaging, and combinations of colours.

Under Indian law, a trademark serves three vital commercial functions: it indicates origin of goods or services, guarantees consistent quality to consumers, and protects commercial goodwill against unlawful misappropriation by third parties.

2. The Spectrum of Distinctiveness: From Coined to Generic

The legal protectability of a brand name is evaluated along the classic spectrum of distinctiveness recognized by Indian courts:

1. Fanciful / Coined / Invented Marks (Strongest Legal Protection)

Words invented purely for trademark use with no pre-existing linguistic meaning in any language. Examples: Kodak, Xerox, Rolex. Coined marks receive immediate, robust protection under Section 9 without any burden to prove acquired distinctiveness.

2. Arbitrary Marks (Highly Protectable)

Real dictionary words applied to goods or services that bear zero logical connection to their common meaning. Examples: Apple for computers, Camel for cigarettes, Blackberry for phones.

3. Suggestive Marks (Protectable Without Secondary Meaning)

Marks that hint at the character or quality of the goods/services through metaphor or imagination, without explicitly describing them. Examples: Netflix (internet movies), Airbus (large airplanes), Swiggy.

4. Descriptive Marks (Prima Facie Unregistrable under Section 9(1)(b))

Marks designating the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, or geographical origin of goods. Example: Cold & Sweet for ice cream. Unregistrable unless the applicant proves extensive prior use and secondary meaning under the Proviso to Section 9(1).

5. Generic Marks (Fatal Objection: Unregistrable)

Common statutory names of the goods or services themselves (e.g. attempting to register Coffee for brewed coffee beans). Section 9(1)(c) bars generic marks absolutely from registration.

3. Word Mark vs. Device Mark: The Strategic Filing Decision

Applicants filing Form TM-A must choose whether to file a Word Mark, a Device Mark (Logo), or both:

Word Mark Protection (Broadest Legal Scope)

Registers the literal letters, words, and phonetic sequence regardless of font, typography, colour, or visual styling.

  • Protects the word across all design iterations and logo redesigns.
  • Protects against phonetic duplicates in speech and writing.
  • Recommended as the primary filing for all core brand names.

Device / Logo Mark Protection

Registers the visual artwork, unique font styling, graphic insignia, and colour combinations as an integrated composite mark.

  • Section 17 Anti-Dissection Rule: Protection applies to the logo as a whole.
  • Essential for visual iconography, app icons, and stylized symbols.
  • Recommended where the underlying word is slightly descriptive but visually distinct.

4. User Claim: "Proposed to be Used" vs. Prior Use (Rule 25)

Under Indian trademark law, rights are established primarily through prior commercial use rather than mere registration (the doctrine of prior user supremacy affirmed by the Supreme Court in S. Syed Mohideen v. P. Sulochana Bai). When drafting Form TM-A, applicants must make a binding legal declaration:

Two Statutory Declarations in Form TM-A:

  • Proposed to be Used: If the brand has not yet launched commercially in India. No prior documentary proof is required. Registration grants protection from the filing date.
  • Claiming Prior Commercial Use (Rule 25 Mandate): If the applicant has already used the mark in India, they may claim an exact historical date (e.g. 15 March 2021). Under Rule 25 of Trade Marks Rules, 2017, claiming prior use mandates the filing of an Affidavit of User attested before a Notary Public alongside supporting commercial exhibits (tax invoices, shipping bills, domain registration records, advertising materials, and CA-certified turnover certificates).

5. The Complete Statutory Lifecycle: Filing to Registration Certificate

From initial filing on Form TM-A to receiving the statutory Registration Certificate under Section 23, an application navigates these formal stages:

1
E-Filing Form TM-A & Application Number Generation (Day 1)

Application is filed electronically on IP India with Class 3 DSC. Official government filing receipt with permanent application number is issued immediately. Applicant secures statutory right to affix the ™ symbol.

2
Formality Check Pass & Substantive Examination (Months 1–3)

The Registry verifies documentation and conducts electronic search under Section 9 (absolute grounds) and Section 11 (conflicting earlier marks). If cleared, mark moves directly to advertisement; otherwise, a First Examination Report (FER) is issued under Rule 33.

3
Examination Reply & Show-Cause Hearing (Strict 30-Day Window)

Under Rule 33(4), the applicant must file a formal legal response within 30 days (1 month). If the reply does not satisfy the Examiner, a virtual show-cause hearing is scheduled before an Assistant Registrar.

4
Journal Advertisement & 4-Month Public Opposition Window (Section 20 & 21)

Accepted marks are advertised in the official weekly Trade Marks Journal. Third parties have exactly 4 months to file a Notice of Opposition in Form TM-O under Section 21.

5
Registration Certificate & Symbol ® Authority (Section 23)

If no opposition is filed (or opposition is dismissed), the Registrar issues the official digital Registration Certificate with dynamic QR code under the seal of the Trade Marks Registry. The owner may now lawfully display the ® symbol.

6. The Law on Trademark Symbols: ™ vs. ® and Section 107 Penalties

Entrepreneurs frequently confuse the statutory symbols. Under Indian law, the distinction is strictly criminal:

The ™ (Trademark Claim) Symbol

Indicates that the owner claims common-law rights or has filed Form TM-A with the Trade Marks Registry. Can be used from Day 1 of application filing to put the market on notice.

The ® (Registered Trademark) Symbol

May be used strictly after the Section 23 Registration Certificate has been officially issued. Using the ® symbol on a pending or unregistered mark is a cognizable criminal offense under Section 107 of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 punishable with imprisonment up to 3 years and fines.
